growth

What if I look back in five years and nothing's changed

Here's the thought that actually keeps me up sometimes, not exams, not money, not even the job market falling apart around every graduate I know. It's picturing myself five years from now, sitting exactly where I am, feeling exactly how I feel right now, and realizing I didn't move an inch.

Goals are supposed to fix that. Set one, hit it, set a bigger one, repeat, keep climbing. Except nobody warns you that the climbing part never actually stops feeling like climbing. I remember being seventeen and completely convinced that getting into university would be the thing, the moment I'd finally exhale. I got in. I did not exhale. I just found a new ledge to stand on and started worrying about the next one.

Poly wasn't easy either, by the way. Years of just showing up, doing the boring unglamorous version of "working hard" that nobody posts about, just to get to this exact point. And now that I'm here? Already restless. Already scanning for what's next like the last few years didn't happen.

So maybe the actual skill isn't wanting less, because clearly I'm going to keep wanting more regardless. Maybe it's just remembering to stop every so often, look back at seventeen-year-old me, and admit: she'd be pretty relieved to see this. That dream she had quietly, the one she never said out loud in case it didn't happen? It kind of already did.

family

The gratitude list always has the same names on it

I have this small habit, not even a proper journaling thing, just a note on my phone, where every now and then I make myself write down three things I'm grateful for. Doesn't matter what kind of week it's been. My family shows up on that list basically every single time, without me even having to think hard about it.

That's the part that gets me. I've been short with them on bad days. Cancelled plans for deadlines, gone quiet for weeks because I was buried in something, forgotten to check in the way I should have. And every time, they're just still there. No lecture about it, no scoreboard being kept. Just there.

It's so easy to treat that as background noise, to assume the people who show up for you will always show up, so you stop noticing the showing up part. Work gets loud, school gets loud, your own head gets loud, and the people who've been quietly consistent the whole time get the least of your attention because they're the safest bet.

So this isn't some big revelation, it's just a note to myself, out loud: whatever's eating up your week right now will still be eating up your week in a month. Your people won't wait around forever for you to notice them. Go be annoying and call them.

new york

I said yes to New York before I let myself think about it properly

If I'd sat with the decision for even a week longer, I probably wouldn't have gone. Some things you have to say yes to fast, before the sensible part of your brain catches up and finds every reason to just stay home.

I'd never set foot in the US before, not once. And this wasn't a group trip with a safety net of familiar faces either, it was NOC, which meant landing alone, finding my own way around, figuring out a city that runs at a completely different speed from anything I grew up with. My family group chat turned into a rotating archive of news headlines the second I told them where I was going: crime rates, safety warnings, someone's cousin's friend who had a bad experience once. I'm not going to sit here and pretend none of that is real, because it is. You do have to be more switched on there, where you walk, when you walk, who knows where you are.

But somewhere between the group chat panic and the actual flight, I made peace with the fact that staying comfortable was never going to teach me anything. You don't find out what you're capable of by avoiding every situation that could go wrong. You find out by going anyway, staying alert instead of staying home, and figuring the rest out in real time.

A year on, I can say it straight: it's one of the best decisions I've made, maybe the best one. Not because nothing scared me, plenty did, but because I didn't let the fear be the one making the decision.

Jun 20
career

Twenty years of school and I still don't know how I'm going to make a living

Some nights the spiral starts the same way. It's late, I should be asleep, and instead I'm doing the math on whether a general business degree was actually a smart call or just the safe, forgettable choice I made at eighteen because I didn't know what else to pick. The economy is not being kind about it either. Half the people I know are sending applications into a void and hearing nothing back for weeks.

This hit different during summer internship season. I lost count of how many applications I sent out, somewhere past fifty, maybe closer to sixty, and heard back from a small handful. The ones that turned into actual interviews felt less like a conversation and more like an audition, sitting across from people who'd clearly seen a dozen other candidates that same week, all of us giving slightly different versions of the same rehearsed answer, all of us fully aware how replaceable we looked on paper.

And somewhere in the middle of that, the thought just arrives, uninvited, usually around midnight: what do you mean this is what you get after twenty years of school? Kindergarten through university, all of it building toward, what, a stack of unanswered applications and a lot of "we've decided to move forward with other candidates" emails?

I don't have a neat answer for this one. I don't think anyone my age does, whatever they're posting online. I just know the question keeps showing up, usually uninvited, usually late, and I'd rather write it down than pretend it isn't there.
